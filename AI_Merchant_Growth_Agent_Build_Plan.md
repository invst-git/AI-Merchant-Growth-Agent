# AI Merchant Growth Agent — Build Plan

## What we are actually building

Track 01 asks for a merchant that can grow its own revenue and make itself sellable to AI buyers, and it grades on one narrow, specific bar: every money action has to be explainable, bounded, and gated, with a visible audit trail and at least one failure handled gracefully. Reading the research, the genuinely open ground is not discovery (ACP, UCP and the big platforms already own that), and it is not recommendations (Nosto, Rebuy and a dozen Shopify apps already ship AI upsell). The open ground is the decision-and-proof layer sitting between them: an agent that looks at a cart, decides whether to upsell, cross-sell, or do nothing at all, in order to maximize expected incremental merchant profit rather than click-through, and that can then show — with real statistics, not a vanity percentage — that its decisions actually created revenue a plain checkout wouldn't have. Wrapped around a real (if thin) agent-to-agent Razorpay Test Mode checkout, that combination is what nobody currently ships as one product.

So the plan below builds one merchant, one catalogue, one growth-decision engine that can say no, one real Razorpay Test Mode payment loop, and one honest offline experiment proving lift — and it deliberately does not build the fuller protocol stacks, the marketplace, or the second recommendation UI that would eat the three to four weeks available without moving the score. Two assumptions shape the schedule below: a small team (roughly two to four people) working close to full-time, and a Python-plus-lightweight-web-backend stack (pandas/scikit-learn/mlxtend for the data and modeling work, FastAPI or a comparable Node backend for the catalogue and payments API, and whatever LLM agent framework the team is already comfortable with for the Buyer and Merchant agents). If the team is smaller or working part-time, stretch every phase's day range roughly proportionally; a solo builder should expect closer to five to six weeks than three.

## Scope discipline: what this plan deliberately excludes

Before the phases, the standing constraints that apply across all of them, because scope creep is the single most likely way this project fails on stage. This build will not implement the full AP2 mandate cryptography, will not touch x402 stablecoin settlement, and will not attempt an NPCI UAP registry integration — all three add regulatory or cryptographic surface with zero marginal value for an INR upsell demo, and AP2 itself is only at v0.1 with human-not-present flows still on its own roadmap. It will not build a multi-merchant marketplace; one merchant and one catalogue is the whole demo. It will not build an LLM-simulated shopper for the experiment, because a language model's guess at a customer's purchase behavior has no grounding in real response rates and would undermine the one thing this project is supposed to win on — honest, defensible evidence. It will not claim or imply a live production A/B result; the offline replay experiment is explicitly one evidence tier below that, and the plan says so out loud rather than hoping nobody asks. And it will not build a second merchandising or recommendation-configuration interface, because that is exactly the ground Nosto, Rebuy, and Dynamic Yield already occupy, and competing with them on widgets is a losing game. Every phase below repeats the specific version of this discipline that applies to it.

## Phase 0 — Foundations and scoping

The first one to two days are about locking the scope above in writing before any code exists, and getting every account and credential ready so nothing blocks later phases. This means writing down the exact objective function the growth engine will optimize — expected incremental contribution equals the probability a customer accepts an offer, times the incremental basket value multiplied by a margin proxy, minus an expected downside for cart abandonment risk — and deciding up front what that margin proxy will be, since none of the candidate datasets contain true cost-of-goods data. It also means registering a Razorpay Test Mode account, generating Test API keys, and standing up the official Razorpay MCP Server, choosing between the hosted version and a self-hosted Docker instance depending on how much control the team wants over the tool surface the agent calls. The repository should be organized from day one into clearly separated pieces — a catalogue service, a decision engine, a cart service, a payments layer, an audit store, an experiment engine, and a dashboard — even if several of them start as a single small codebase rather than separate deployed services. The audit record schema (what gets logged for every intent, decision, cart change, and payment) should be sketched now, because every later phase writes into it. This phase is done when a trivial order can be created and captured end-to-end in Razorpay Test Mode, the objective function and margin proxy are written down in one page, and the audit schema exists as a draft. The guardrail here is architectural restraint: no protocol libraries beyond the one thin catalogue endpoint planned for Phase 3, no microservice sprawl, and no user-account or authentication system beyond what the demo script actually needs.

## Phase 1 — Data acquisition and preprocessing

With scope locked, the next three to four days go into getting the three datasets into an analysis-ready state. dunnhumby's Complete Journey is the primary source: the transaction_data table (household, basket, day, product, quantity, sales value, store, and the retailer and coupon discount fields) needs a computed loyalty price per line — sales value minus the sum of the retail and coupon-match discounts, divided by quantity — because sales value alone is what the retailer received, not what the customer paid. The product table's three-level category hierarchy (department, commodity, sub-commodity, brand, manufacturer) is what will drive complement and substitute reasoning later, so it needs to be joined cleanly onto every transaction. The household demographic table and the campaign, campaign-description, coupon, and coupon-redemption tables come along as a weak but real marketing-contact signal, useful later for the acceptance model. Olist comes in second, mainly for its order-items table (price and freight value per line), its payments table (method and installment count), and its seller and review tables — this adds marketplace and payment-mix realism, but because most Olist orders are single-item, it is not the primary source of cross-sell signal. UCI's Online Retail II comes in third and stays independent: its invoice, stock code, quantity, price, and customer fields, with invoices prefixed "C" (cancellations) dropped, serve purely as a second, unrelated basket set to confirm that whatever affinity rules get learned from dunnhumby actually generalize. This phase ends with one clean, documented basket table per dataset. The guardrail is to resist the urge to build a general ETL pipeline or scheduler for what is, in the end, three static files — plain scripts or notebooks are enough — and to resist chasing true margin data that does not exist in any of these sources; commit early to a stated, transparent margin-proxy assumption instead.

## Phase 2 — The affinity and decision engine

This is the four to five day phase that builds the actual intelligence, independent of any agent or payment plumbing, so it can be validated on its own before anything else depends on it. Market-basket mining — Apriori or FP-Growth — run over the dunnhumby baskets produces candidate product-pair rules scored by support, confidence, and lift, keeping only rules with lift meaningfully above one (roughly 1.5 or higher) as genuine complements, with the category hierarchy from Phase 1 used to identify upsell tiers within the same sub-commodity. Alongside that, a simple offer-acceptance model — logistic regression is deliberately the right choice here, not a heavier model, because it stays interpretable and defensible under the Track's explainability requirement — predicts the probability a given customer accepts a given offer, using price-sensitivity proxies, category affinity, current basket state, and the campaign and coupon history as a weak treatment signal. Those two pieces feed directly into the objective function defined in Phase 0: every candidate action (a specific upsell, a specific cross-sell, or doing nothing) gets scored by expected incremental contribution, and the policy picks whichever scores highest — critically, "no action" is scored on the same footing as every other candidate, not used as a fallback when nothing else fits. Before this phase closes, the engine should be validated offline against held-out dunnhumby baskets, entirely separate from any agent or UI work. The exit criterion is that, given any basket, the engine returns a ranked decision with an attached expected-value score and the specific reason behind it (which rule fired, what the acceptance probability was, what the margin proxy contributed) — that reason is exactly what the audit trail in Phase 6 will surface. The guardrail is to stay away from deep learning or online/real-time learning; a static, offline-trained, explainable model is both easier to defend under questioning and less risky to demo.

## Phase 3 — The agent layer and the agent-readable catalogue

With a validated decision engine in hand, the next three to four days wrap it in an actual agent conversation and build the one piece of "sellable to AI buyers" that this plan commits to. That piece is a single, thin, agent-readable catalogue endpoint, shaped like the feeds used by the Agentic Commerce Protocol — plain JSON exposing product identifiers, price, availability, attributes, declared complements and alternatives, current offers, and a marker of checkout capability — queryable in natural language. The Merchant Agent sits behind it: an LLM-orchestrated component that interprets a buyer's request against the catalogue, selects the base product, calls the Phase 2 decision engine for the growth decision, and presents whatever offer results (which may be no offer at all) for accept or decline. A Buyer Agent is built alongside it purely to make the demo genuinely agent-to-agent rather than a human clicking through a UI — it needs exactly one tool, the ability to query the catalogue in natural language. Cart state needs to be tracked as an explicit before-and-after pair from this point forward, since that diff is what the dashboard will show later. This phase closes when a scripted natural-language request produces a correct catalogue match, a growth decision from the engine (with at least one rehearsed run that resolves to no action, to prove the policy isn't just an always-upsell reflex), and an updated cart — all without any money changing hands yet. The guardrail is firm: no OAuth-based delegated-authentication flows, no cryptographic mandates, no `.well-known` discovery stack, and no attempt at full ACP or UCP conformance — one JSON endpoint shaped correctly is the entire "buyer-readiness" commitment, and it stays that way on purpose, because the growth-and-proof half is where the score actually comes from.

## Phase 4 — Razorpay Test Mode integration

This three to four day phase turns an accepted offer into a real, verifiable transaction, and it is where the strongest Razorpay-specific narrative lives. The server creates a Razorpay Order through the Orders API for the final basket amount in paise, carrying basket metadata in the receipt or notes fields, and — this is the highest-leverage move available — that call is made through the official Razorpay MCP Server's tools (create_order, create_payment_link, fetch_payment, and capture) rather than hand-rolled REST calls, so the Merchant Agent is demonstrably calling Razorpay's own agentic tool surface rather than merely hitting an API behind the scenes. Standard Checkout completes against Razorpay's mock bank or UPI page, returning a razorpay_order_id, razorpay_payment_id, and razorpay_signature, and the server must verify that signature with HMAC-SHA256 before treating the payment as legitimate, then capture (or confirm auto-capture) and fetch the payment to confirm final status. One webhook listener for the payment.captured event, verifying the x-razorpay-signature header, closes the loop server-side. A second, deliberate path uses the "failure@razorpay" mock UPI identifier to force a decline on command, and that failure needs to be handled visibly and gracefully — a clear message, no half-updated cart or order state, and a logged failed attempt in the audit trail — because this is literally the "one failure handled gracefully" line item in the Track's own bar. A spend cap, enforced here, is the concrete mechanism behind the word "bounded." This phase ends with one successful and one deliberately failed Test Mode payment, both fully traceable back to the specific agent decision that triggered them. The guardrail is to borrow only the concept of a signed, bounded authorization record for the audit log, not the AP2 specification itself, and to stay entirely within Test Mode — no Live Mode keys, no real payment methods, anywhere near this build.

## Phase 5 — The experiment engine (Control versus Agent)

Running in parallel with Phase 6, this three to four day phase produces the actual proof of incremental revenue, and it is deliberately the most methodologically careful part of the build. A data-grounded simulator — not a language model pretending to be a shopper — assigns each simulated customer a price sensitivity, a purchase frequency, a category preference, a historical basket, and an acceptance probability, all derived from the real distributions in the dunnhumby and UCI data prepared in Phase 1. Two matched populations are drawn from that same underlying distribution: a Control population that goes straight from cart to checkout with no intervention, and an Agent population that runs through the full Phase 2 decision engine, no-action option included, before checkout. From those two runs, the build computes conversion rate, average order value, items per order, attach rate, revenue per session, and incremental revenue and incremental AOV in both absolute and percentage terms, with the margin proxy applied to get a contribution-margin view rather than a raw revenue view. The statistics need to hold up under questioning: a two-sample test appropriate to typically skewed basket values (a t-test or Mann-Whitney), bootstrap confidence intervals around the incremental-revenue estimate, and an uplift or Qini curve (or area-under-the-uplift-curve) comparing the learned policy against both a random-offer baseline and an offer-to-everyone baseline, to prove the targeting itself is doing work, not just the act of offering something. Every result gets explicitly labeled against the three evidence tiers the whole pitch rests on: this offline replay is tier (a), empirically validated; the one live Razorpay transaction from Phase 4 is tier (b), demonstrated; and a real merchant A/B test remains tier (c), future validation, stated as such rather than implied to already exist. The guardrail, repeated from the top-level scope section because it matters most here, is no LLM-simulated shopper population and no language anywhere that could be read as claiming a production result.

## Phase 6 — Audit trail, bounding, and gating

Running alongside Phase 5 over two to three days, this phase formalizes what has been logged informally since Phase 0 into the one artifact the dashboard and the pitch will both point to under questioning. The audit record schema gets finalized end to end: the buyer's original intent, the decision made against it (which rule fired, what the acceptance model estimated, what the expected value was, and specifically including cases where the chosen action was no action at all), the cart before and after, the payment record (order id, payment id, whether the signature verified, captured or failed), and the final outcome. "Bounded" gets enforced as two concrete rules rather than a slogan: the spend cap from Phase 4, and a restriction that the agent may only offer SKUs the catalogue has declared as complements or alternatives, never an arbitrary upsell. "Gated" means an explicit accept step is required before any Razorpay order gets created — nothing charges silently. "Explainable" means every single decision record carries a plain-English reason, generated from the same rule, probability, and expected-value numbers the engine already computed in Phase 2, so it can be read back on the dashboard without translation. This phase is done when any transaction in the system, successful or failed, can be traced start to finish using only the audit log. The guardrail is to resist building a general compliance or policy engine — one structured log table plus the two enforced rules above is exactly what "bounded and gated" requires for this bar, and nothing more elaborate earns extra credit.

## Phase 7 — Dashboard and demo assembly

The three to four days here assemble everything built so far into the single screen the jury will actually watch, reading live from the audit log and the Phase 5 experiment output rather than from any hand-curated numbers. A per-transaction view shows the original basket against the final basket, the offer that was made and the plain-English reason behind it, the payment status, and a link into the raw audit record. An aggregate view shows the Control-versus-Agent metrics from Phase 5 — AOV, attach rate, incremental AOV percentage, incremental revenue percentage — with their confidence intervals and the uplift curve rendered visibly, not just tabulated. Every figure involving money or lift carries an explicit "Test Mode / simulated" label, consistently, because the honesty of that labeling is a real part of the credibility strategy, not decoration. The exact live-demo sequence gets scripted and rehearsed here: a natural-language buyer request, a cross-sell offer accepted and paid, a second run where the engine chooses no action to prove the policy isn't reflexive, one deliberately triggered failed payment handled gracefully, a walkthrough of the dashboard's two views, and a close on the three evidence tiers. This phase ends when that sequence runs start to finish twice in a row without any manual data patching in between. The guardrail is to not build a second merchandising or recommendation-configuration interface — the dashboard's only job is to prove the numbers and narrate the audit trail, not to compete with Nosto or Rebuy on UI features.

## Phase 8 — Hardening, rehearsal, and pitch prep

The final two to three days are explicitly not for building anything new. The full demo sequence gets dry-run repeatedly on the actual network and environment that will be used live, with a recorded backup video kept in case anything fails during the real thing. The one-sentence thesis and the pre-emptive answers to the three most likely jury objections — that simulated lift isn't real lift, that this is just another Nosto or Rebuy, and where the margin data actually comes from — get written directly into speaker notes rather than improvised. A final pass checks the standing scope-discipline list from the top of this plan: no AP2 mandates crept in, no x402, no UAP registry, no multi-merchant marketplace, no LLM-shopper simulator, no live A/B claim anywhere in the language, no protocol conformance suite, and no second recommendation UI. This phase closes with two clean back-to-back rehearsals, a backup recording, and a written objection-and-answer sheet. The guardrail for this phase is the simplest one in the whole plan: nothing gets built here, only hardened and rehearsed.

## Why this ordering

Phases 1 and 2 (data and the decision engine) can start immediately after Phase 0 and run somewhat independently of Phases 3 and 4 (agents and payments) until they meet inside the Merchant Agent — that is the main place a team can compress the three-week estimate down toward two by splitting into a data/modeling track and a Razorpay-integration track from day two onward. Phases 5 and 6 are placed after the agent and payment plumbing exists because both the experiment engine and the audit trail need real decisions and real transactions to log and measure, but they do not depend on each other and can run side by side. Everything converges in Phase 7, and Phase 8 is held back on purpose as pure rehearsal time, because the research is explicit that a working end-to-end demo with honest numbers beats a more ambitious build that breaks on stage.
